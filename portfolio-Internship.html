Internship Story

Commissioning & Efficiency Analysis of a PEM Electrolyzer

Eight months working with hydrogen production systems in Germany — from initial setup to understanding what makes an electrolyzer truly efficient.

Duration May — December 2024
Location Karlsruhe, Germany
Organization Hochschule Karlsruhe (HKA)
Supervisor Prof. Dr.-Ing. Maurice Kettner
01 — Context

The Project & The Place

When I arrived at Hochschule Karlsruhe in May 2024, I joined the CO2InnO project — a real-laboratory initiative exploring the transition to a green hydrogen economy. The project brings together universities and industry partners across the Upper Rhine region to develop practical solutions for carbon-neutral innovation.

My specific focus was on a Proton Exchange Membrane (PEM) electrolyzer — a device that uses electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. The hydrogen produced can be stored and later used in fuel cells to generate electricity, or as a clean fuel for industrial processes.

The electrolyzer had been partially assembled, but it needed commissioning, testing, and optimization. The broader goal was to understand how efficiently it could operate under variable power inputs — particularly important for integrating with renewable energy sources like solar and wind, which don't provide constant power.

CO2InnO Consortium Partners — Co-financed by Interreg Oberrhein under the European Regional Development Fund (€2,556,522)

02 — The System

Understanding the Setup

Before I could optimize anything, I needed to understand every component of the system. The PEM electrolyzer is elegant in concept but complex in implementation. Water goes in, electricity is applied, and hydrogen comes out — but controlling this process safely and efficiently requires careful orchestration of multiple subsystems.

Complete system schematic showing water supply, electrolyzer stack, and gas handling
AC-DC rectifier converting grid power to the regulated DC needed by the stack
Siemens TIA Portal — where the control logic lives

The heart of the system is the PEM stack itself — a series of membrane electrode assemblies where the actual electrochemical reaction happens. Surrounding it are supporting systems: deionized water supply, KO pots (knock-out vessels) for gas-liquid separation, cooling systems, and safety mechanisms to prevent hydrogen backflow.

03 — The Challenge

What We Needed to Solve

The primary challenge was understanding and optimizing the electrolyzer's efficiency across its entire operating range. Most electrolyzers are tested at steady-state conditions, but real-world renewable energy inputs fluctuate constantly. We needed to know: How does efficiency change when power input varies from 20% to 100%?

This breaks down into three key metrics:

  • Faraday Efficiency — How much of the electrical current actually produces hydrogen vs. side reactions
  • Voltage Efficiency — How close the actual cell voltage is to the theoretical minimum
  • Overall System Efficiency — The combined measure of energy input vs. hydrogen energy content

Additionally, we needed reliable measurement systems. Hydrogen flow measurement proved particularly tricky — it's a low-density gas, and small measurement errors could significantly affect efficiency calculations.

04 — What I Did

Programming, Measuring & Troubleshooting

My work fell into three main areas: control system programming, instrumentation setup, and experimental data collection. Each presented its own learning curve.

Control System Programming

I programmed a Siemens S7-1200 PLC using TIA Portal to manage the electrolyzer's operation. This involved creating logic for valve sequencing, pump control, safety interlocks, and emergency shutdowns. The PLC also handled data logging — recording current, voltage, temperature, pressure, and flow rates at regular intervals.

Bronkhorst FlowSuite software for hydrogen flow measurement
WM271 power meter for precise electrical measurements

Instrumentation & Troubleshooting

I integrated a Bronkhorst El-Flow Prestige FG-201CV mass flow meter via Modbus communication for precise hydrogen flow measurement. This involved understanding Modbus protocols, configuring the meter's settings, and verifying calibration.

Along the way, we encountered practical problems. At around 60% power, water started leaking from the KO pots. Through investigation, I discovered this was a pressure-related issue — the system needed a back-pressure regulator to maintain consistent pressure in the hydrogen line. Installing and tuning this regulator solved the problem.

05 — The Experiments

From 20% to 100% Power

With the system stable and instruments calibrated, I designed an experimental protocol. We would run the electrolyzer at power levels from 20% to 100% in 5% increments, holding each level steady for sufficient time to reach thermal equilibrium before recording data.

Each test run generated data on current, voltage, hydrogen flow rate, temperature, and pressure. Over several weeks, I built a comprehensive dataset mapping the electrolyzer's performance across its full operating envelope.

06 — Analysis

Finding Patterns in the Data

Raw data is just numbers. The interesting work was in processing and analyzing it to extract meaningful insights. I used Python with Pandas and Matplotlib to automate the analysis pipeline.

Data visualization using Pandas and Matplotlib
Tafel plot for electrochemical analysis
Overall efficiency comparison across power levels

The analysis revealed that efficiency wasn't constant — it peaked around 25% power input and gradually decreased as power increased. This inverse relationship between power and efficiency is characteristic of PEM electrolyzers, where activation losses dominate at lower currents but ohmic losses become more significant at higher currents.

I also constructed Tafel plots to determine the exchange current density and activation energy — fundamental electrochemical parameters that characterize the reaction kinetics at the electrode surfaces. These values help compare different catalyst materials and operating conditions.

07 — Results

What We Learned

The internship produced both practical outcomes and deeper understanding:

  • A fully commissioned and documented PEM electrolyzer system ready for ongoing research
  • A comprehensive performance map showing efficiency across the full operating range
  • Python automation tools for data processing that can be used in future experiments
  • Identification of optimal operating points for different use cases
  • Recommendations for control strategy improvements

08 — Reflection

What I Take Away

This internship taught me that engineering is as much about problem-solving mindset as technical knowledge. When water leaked from the KO pots, there wasn't a chapter in a textbook labeled "How to Fix This Specific Leak." I had to observe, hypothesize, test, and iterate — the scientific method in real time.

I also gained appreciation for the complexity of energy systems. A PEM electrolyzer seems simple in concept, but making it work efficiently requires understanding electrochemistry, thermodynamics, control systems, and materials science. Renewable energy integration adds another layer of complexity — the systems of the future need to handle variability gracefully.

Beyond the technical skills — PLC programming, Python data analysis, instrumentation setup — I developed confidence in working independently, communicating across language barriers (the working language was German), and persisting through setbacks. Some experiments failed. Some approaches didn't work. That's part of the process.

I'm grateful to Prof. Dr.-Ing. Maurice Kettner and Mr. Beerlage for their mentorship, and to the CO2InnO consortium for the opportunity to contribute to meaningful research in the transition to sustainable energy.
